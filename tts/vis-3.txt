Stop three. The great crown heist.

Welcome to the upper castle. For long stretches of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the most precious object in Hungary was kept right here: the Holy Crown. And in Hungary, the crown wasn't just jewellery. Without it, a coronation didn't really count. Whoever held the crown, held the kingdom.

Which brings us to a winter night in fourteen forty. The king has just died. His widow, Queen Elizabeth, is pregnant, and many of the Hungarian nobles want to give the throne to someone else. If her baby is a boy, she needs him crowned. Fast. And for that, she needs the crown. The problem? It's locked up in this castle, guarded by men she can't count on.

So, she asks her lady-in-waiting, Helene Kottanner, to steal it.

On a February night, Helene and an accomplice slip into the castle. While she keeps watch, terrified, he works his way through the locks to the chamber where the crown is kept. They take the crown, hide it inside a pillow, and then, as a final touch, they lock the doors behind them and put the seals back. As if nothing had happened.

Then comes the escape. It's winter, and the Danube is frozen. Helene rides a sledge across the ice, with the crown beside her, listening to the ice, and hoping it holds.

It holds. The crown reaches the queen, and, according to Helene, the baby boy is born within the same hour. A few months later, little Ladislaus is crowned King of Hungary, as a baby, with the stolen crown. And Helene herself holds him during the ceremony.

How do we know all this? Because years later, Helene told the whole story in her memoirs. So this is a medieval heist, told by the thief herself.

One story even says that the little cross on top of the crown got bent during the escape. Whatever the reason, it's still crooked today. If you visit the Parliament in Budapest, you can see it for yourself.
