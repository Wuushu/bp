Gellért Hill in three minutes.

You're on a lump of hard dolomite rock, about a hundred and thirty metres above the Danube. It doesn't look like much from below, but this hill is packed with stories: a murdered bishop, a coven of witches, a fortress that pointed its cannons at its own city, and a statue that quietly switched sides. So... let's start at the bottom.

Actually, let's start underneath the bottom. Budapest sits on a fault line, a crack between the hills of Buda and the flat plain of Pest. Rainwater sinks deep into the ground, gets heated by the Earth, and rises back up along that crack, right at the foot of this hill. That's why there's a thermal bath down there, and why Budapest calls itself the City of Spas. The hill is basically a lid on a giant underground kettle.

Now, the name. The year is ten forty-six. Hungary is a brand-new Christian kingdom, and not everyone is thrilled about it. A bishop from Venice called Gerard, Gellért in Hungarian, had come to help King Stephen convert the country. He even tutored the king's son. But after Stephen dies, a pagan uprising breaks out, and according to legend, the rebels sealed Gellért in a spiked barrel and rolled him down this hill into the river. If you walked up from the Elisabeth Bridge, you passed him: the big statue holding up a cross, above a waterfall.

For centuries after that, the hill had a spooky reputation. Folk tales said this was where Hungary's witches gathered for their midnight meetings. Less spooky: the slopes were covered in vineyards, until a tiny insect called phylloxera wiped them out in the late eighteen hundreds.

Then comes the fortress on top. In eighteen forty-eight, Hungary rises up against the Habsburgs, and the revolution is crushed the following year. So the Habsburgs build the Citadella, with its guns aimed not at an invading army, but at Budapest itself. The message was simple: we're watching you. Today it's a park, recently reopened after a long renovation.

And then there's her. The Liberty Statue: a woman holding a palm leaf high above the city, unveiled in nineteen forty-seven. She was put up to celebrate the Soviet army's capture of Budapest, with a Soviet soldier standing guard at her feet. After communism fell, the soldier was moved to a statue park, and the inscription was rewritten to honour everyone who gave their lives for Hungary's independence, freedom and prosperity. Same statue. New meaning.

One last thing. On the way down, look for a door in the rock near the baths. That's the Cave Church, opened by Pauline monks in nineteen twenty-six. In nineteen fifty-one, the communist government arrested the monks and sealed the entrance with a thick concrete wall. It stayed shut until the wall came down in nineteen eighty-nine.

So: a murdered bishop, witches, wine, a fortress aimed the wrong way, and a statue that changed its mind. All on one hill. And the reward for climbing it? The best view in Budapest. Every bridge, the Parliament, the Castle across the water. Stay for sunset, and watch the whole city switch on.
